Why You Should Not Use AI Coding Assistants if You Are A Beginner

Random thoughts of a guy who isn’t trying to sell you a product, course, or your analytics

AI coding assistants like Copilot, Claude and ChatGPT are amazing and very helpful for software developers. You also should stop using them if you’re a junior or learning to program for the first time.

To explain my reasoning, I’ll tell you a story about of my early days as an engineering student at the University of Cape Town. One of the courses I had to take was an introduction to object-oriented programing using Java. The course itself was very fun and helped me begin my formal journey to mastery which led me to where I am today.

During one of our classes, our lecturer told a bad joke that ended up being prophetic. To give some context, we were required to use a painfully simple and very specific code editor. All it provided was syntax highlighting, a terminal to run your commands, and the official Java class docs which were basically useless. We had no autocomplete, no linting, no helpful popups that explained what each function did, nothing. Quite frankly, it was a horrible experience coding in it if you’re used to even a basic Visual Studio Code setup.

You could use whatever code editor you wanted for assignments so long as your code worked and you weren’t cheating. Our lecturer strongly advised against this though. He recommended we use that abomination of an editor because that’s what we would use for all our programming tests. He also mentioned it was very beneficial for our written tests too. When we asked how using that editor was useful for our written tests he said.

If you keep using your fancy IDEs, when you reach the code section of the exam, you’re gonna put a dot on the paper, and nothing is going to pop up.

Now, why did I tell you this story? Well firstly, that joke aged like wine in my mind so I wanted to share it some more. More importantly, our lecturer wasn’t lying. Many people ignored his advice, and they had a very difficult time like he mentioned. But why was that? Were they just lazy? Were they not smart enough to understand what was being taught? Obviously, my classmates were neither lazy or dumb. Every single one of them was incredibly intelligent and hard working. No, the thing that got them was their habits.

Our brains are very good at optimising. Because of this, when we used that awefull code editor, we were forced to do more work mentally. This had the benefit of making us understand core principles more deeply. The downside was it was inconvenient and painful at first until we adapted.

Now, am I saying you should stop using modern code editors and AI coding assistants? No. What I am saying is that its very beneficial for you to avoid these things when you are still inexperienced in the field. The purpose of tools like CoPilot is to assist you and make you more productive. The thing is, though, if you are a beginner, you will inevitably stop seeing these tools as a helper and more as your source of truth.

To be blunt, you don’t know whats good or bad yet. Writing good working software isn’t just about writing code. Yes, writing code is still important but, if you are working on any sufficiently complex system, its not the most important part. When someone tells you to build a Twitter clone or something similar, what they want to see is how you think. How are you going to choose your tools? How will you prioritise features? What are the trade-offs you’re making? How do you fix bugs that you’ll inevitably introduce? How do you work around time, hardware, storage and other constraints?

I can go on and on, but I hope you get my point. Using AI code tools is great if you already know what you want and don’t want to go through the hassle of writing generic boilerplate code. Its also great if you want to quickly search something you know how to do but forgot a little piece. If however, you don’t already know what you are doing and you just blindly trust these tools, you are setting yourself up for failure.

Look away from the wildly optimistic/pessimistic side of the internet preaching about AI taking our jobs and doing everything a junior developer can do. You’ll notice an interesting trend regarding these AI code tools. The first point is they struggle a lot whenever you want very specific solutions. This should make sense when you remember how these tools were created. They are very good at generalizing but not so much when you need novel solutions. Another problem often noted by people who use more advanced tools like Claude is after your codebase has reached a certain size, these models collapse and start doing random things when you ask them to fix the mistakes they made or work on changes across multiple files.

These tools aren’t perfect. This doesn’t mean they are useless. It means you need to know how to use them well. Think of the driving assistants in cars or the flight assistants in planes. They are incredibly convenient but that doesn’t mean the driver or pilot can sleep on the wheel and expect everything to be ok.

Maybe AI tools will reach that point in the future, but they haven’t yet, and you are actively sabotaging yourself if you try to pretend they have. If you are still early in your career, you need that challenge. You need to push your comfort zone and wrestle with a few bugs. I’m not gonna say its fun, but it is important. This is how you will grow and get better at your craft.

Having something that's convenient feels amazing. No one likes fixing bugs or writing boilerplate code for the 10th time. But, that’s how you gain experience, and there’s no shortcut. Once you have the experience, you’ll ironically be waaay better at using these AI code tools.

I hope this article helped you understand why you shouldn’t rely on code days as a junior or beginner. If you disagree with my take, feel free to leave a comment highlighting what I might have missed or where I’m talking rubbish 😁. Whatever the case, I wish you well in your coding adventure.
