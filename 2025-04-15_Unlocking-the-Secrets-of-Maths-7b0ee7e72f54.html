Unlocking the Secrets of Maths

My journey from struggling to acing it

Recently, I’ve been learning about creating educational videos online. That got me thinking — hey, I’m actually pretty good at maths. Why not make a maths course?

As I was planning out the content, I started reflecting on how I became good at maths in the first place. It reminded me of a story — one I’d usually tell people who know me. If you know me, you probably know the story already. But since you don’t, I figured I’d share it here.

I’m now very good at maths. But it wasn’t always the case. I wasn’t a failing student or anything, but I wasn’t getting outstanding marks either. I was just… average. Sometimes even below average.

For a long time, I genuinely believed that was just how I was. I thought

“This is the limit of how good I can be at maths.”

It sucked. I didn’t enjoy the subject. I felt like I was giving it my all, but some people were just naturally gifted — they got maths. I didn’t. I was envious.

But it all changed in high school.

Let me give a bit of context. I grew up in Zimbabwe, where boarding schools are quite common. A lot of kids go to schools where they live on campus, away from home. I like the boarding school setup. It gives children space to discover who they are outside the home environment and find their identity in society.

In my family, boarding school was kind of a tradition. So naturally, I ended up in one as well. In the early years, you don’t usually get to pick your roommates — you’re just assigned. Sometimes, it’s one roommate, but more often, it’s a dorm with 10 to 15 others.

I think the setup is great. You meet new people, build friendships, and eventually, in later years, you can choose your roommates.

That’s how I met someone who would later become a good friend — and, indirectly, change my life.

We were in Form 3 at the time. I noticed he was incredibly good at maths. Ridiculously good. He was always getting full marks or only missing a couple of questions. For a while, I just watched in awe (and, if I’m honest, a bit of envy). I never said anything — pride, right?

But eventually, I couldn’t help myself. I asked him

“How are you so good at maths?”

He told me a story.

Back in primary school, he had been struggling with maths too. His older brother gave him a textbook for the next grade level. He said, “If you want to be good at maths, solve every question in this book. I’m not going to help you.”

It sounded impossible at the time. He struggled — a lot. Sometimes, he could only answer one question a day. Some questions took him days to figure out. Eventually, something clicked. His brain started understanding maths on a deeper level — because it had to. There was no guide, no teacher — just him, struggling, learning, and slowly figuring things out.

He kept up the habit: always studying a year ahead. By the time he got to high school, he was an absolute beast at maths.

I was like, “That’s amazing. How can I be like you?”

At our school, during Form 3, we followed the Cambridge system. There was an optional subject called Additional Mathematics. It’s like a bridge between O-Level and A-Level maths — advanced but still manageable. If you were good at maths, it was a great challenge and a nice primer for what was coming.

He gave me the Additional Mathematics textbook and similarly said

“Answer every question in this book. Start with chapter one. Don’t skip.”

I thought, That’s insane. Every question?

But I decided to give it a try.

Chapter one was on functions. For some reason, I just couldn’t wrap my head around the concept. I struggled hard — so much so that I couldn’t answer even the first question. I spent the entire day wrestling with it. My brain felt like it was on fire, like things were literally shifting inside my head from the effort.

I failed. Completely. But I didn’t stop.

I kept thinking about the problem the next day. By the end of the second day, I finally got the answer. It was such a simple question, really. I don’t even remember it now, but back then, it felt like trying to climb Mount Everest.

But I loved the feeling of conquering something that once felt impossible.

So, I tried the next question. I failed again. But just like before, after about 24 hours of chewing on it, I figured it out.

Then, I kept going.

One question at a time. One chapter at a time. Slowly but surely, I was answering questions way above my level. I had no right to be doing this work — but I was doing it anyway.

Somewhere along the way, maths clicked for me.

Suddenly, all those O-Level problems that used to scare me became obvious. I could see the answers. I wasn’t guessing — I knew.

I stopped taking the subject so seriously, in a weird way. I’d make bets with my friend about who could finish the math tests first. Sometimes, I’d finish 30 minutes early; he’d finish even earlier. We were just that confident in our answers.

Sometimes, I’d even take a separate sheet and mark my paper before handing it in. I understood the marking scheme so well, I could predict how the examiner would score each response.

But I would never’ve gotten to that point without struggling first. That initial failure, trying to learn without relying solely on a teacher — that’s what changed everything.

I used to think the teacher was the only one who had the answers. While teachers are important, you shouldn’t strip yourself of agency. You have the capacity to learn far more than you think.

So yeah, that’s how I got good at maths.

I did the hard questions. I failed. I believed in myself. I chipped away at it, bit by bit. Eventually, people started looking at me like I was the naturally gifted one.

They forgot we used to be on the same level

When I told them how I did it, most of them wouldn’t believe me. Because let’s be real — sitting with a single question for an entire day is hard. It’s humbling. It bruises the ego.

But, to borrow a Christian phrase

Humble yourselves in the presence of the Lord, and He will exalt you.
— James 4:10

What matters is that I humbled myself. I accepted where I was. Because of this, I could grow and improve.

So don’t underestimate where you’re starting from. Don’t underestimate what you already have. You can get better. You just have to be willing to accept where you are now so you can figure out how to get to where you want to be.
