I don’t like the word genius.

It’s strange, even writing it feels uncomfortable. Like saying something I shouldn’t. I’ve never thought of myself that way — even when others did. Maybe especially then.

Growing up, people told me I was smart. Teachers, family, and even strangers sometimes. “He’s gifted.” “He’s a genius.” “He just understands things.” And over time, those words stuck. Not in the way praise usually does — warm and affirming — but in a way that felt… heavy.

Like something I had to live up to. Like a role I didn’t audition for but now had to play, perfectly, without mistakes.

The Silent Weight of Expectations

I did well in school, that much is true. I took eleven subjects at IGCSE O-Level because I was curious about maths. I got distinctions. I got the best mark in my country, Zimbabwe, for Computer Science in my A Levels. I worked hard — really hard — but it never quite felt like the work mattered.

People would smile and say

“Of course, he got top marks. He’s a genius.”

And just like that, all the effort, the hours of study, the late nights, the doubt — it disappeared. The work became invisible. The outcome became inevitable.

It felt like I wasn’t allowed to be tired. Or confused. Or ordinary. Because when you’re the “genius,” you’re expected to always know. To always succeed. To never be unsure.

But I am unsure. More often than I let on.

The Quiet Fear of Being Seen

I started noticing something over the years: I would begin projects and never finish them. Not because I couldn’t — but because I was afraid of what finishing would mean. What it might reveal. What it might invite.

And beneath that fear was something I didn’t want to admit: I was avoiding attention. I’d had enough of being the example. The one people pointed to. The one who was supposed to “have it all together.”

It made it harder to connect. Harder to just be around people without feeling like I was being watched or measured. Harder to be vulnerable — because the moment someone found out about my “track record,” the way they looked at me shifted.

It still happens. And it still hurts, in quiet ways.

Genius Isn’t Armor

I’ve never liked labels, but this one in particular always felt isolating. It implied I had everything figured out, that I didn’t struggle, that I didn’t need help.

But I do. I struggle all the time — just maybe not in visible ways.

I’ve had to learn that being good at something doesn’t mean you’re good at everything. And it certainly doesn’t mean you’re invulnerable.

There are fields where I feel confident — programming, engineering, and science. But there are also things I’m terrible at. I can’t draw to save my life. I find art beautiful, but my hands won’t cooperate. That doesn’t stop me from loving it.

That’s what I wish more people understood. Being good in one area doesn’t cancel out the rest of who you are. You’re still a whole person, with strengths and flaws and soft spots.

The Label Isn’t the Person

I don’t know who this post is for — maybe someone who’s been called a genius one too many times. Maybe someone who’s wondering why they feel so disconnected despite doing everything “right.” Maybe just me, trying to put things into words I’ve avoided for too long.

All I can say is: you’re not alone.

You’re allowed to feel the weight of the word. You’re allowed to question it. And you’re allowed to grow beyond it.

So the next time you think about calling someone a genius, pause. Ask yourself if what you’re trying to do is see them… or sort them.

And if you’re the one carrying that label — I hope you know it’s okay to put it down sometimes. You’re still you, even without it. Maybe more so.
